Entry & safety routine 9 pts

  • Knock and ask permission to enterScan as you walk in: safety hazards (spills, cords, rails), signs of distress (breathing hard, grimacing, confused).
  • Hand hygieneFoam in on entry, foam out on exit. Say it out loud.
  • Introduce self with AIDETAcknowledge · Introduce (name, role) · Duration ("about 10 minutes") · Explanation ("I'll check your heart and lungs") · Thank you.
  • Assess general appearance, grooming, mood, speech, ability to respondSay it: "Patient is well groomed, calm, speaking in full clear sentences, responds appropriately."
  • Ensure HIPAA / privacyClose the door or curtain; ask visitors to step out or ask permission to talk in front of them.
  • Verify armband: name + date of birth; note LOCAsk the patient to state their name and DOB, then compare to the armband and chart. Never read it to them.
  • Assess allergies and precautionsAsk about drug, food, latex allergies and the reaction; check allergy band and isolation signage. All four charts say "none". Confirm anyway.
  • Finish within 10 minutes
  • Leave the room safeCall light in reach, tray table next to bed, bed in lowest position, 2 side rails up.

Focused history 1 pt

Assess pain using COLDSPA or OPQRST, then ask about associated symptoms. All four patients have a pain score above 0, so this applies every time.

OPQRST
  • Onset: when did it start? sudden or gradual?
  • Provocation / palliation: what makes it worse or better?
  • Quality: sharp, dull, aching, burning, pressure?
  • Region / radiation: where, and does it spread?
  • Severity: 0–10 now, at worst, acceptable goal
  • Timing / treatment: constant or comes and goes; what have you tried?
COLDSPA
  • Character
  • Onset
  • Location
  • Duration
  • Severity
  • Pattern (what helps, what worsens)
  • Associated factors

General focused physical assessment 5 pts

  • Orientation to person, place, time, situation, and say the finding"Alert and oriented ×4."
  • Inspect general appearance, LOC, skin colorNote pallor, cyanosis (lips, nail beds), flushing, diaphoresis.
  • Feel temperature and moisture of upper and lower extremities, then say itBack of your hand, compare sides. "Skin warm, dry, intact bilaterally upper and lower."
  • Use equipment correctlyClean stethoscope; diaphragm for high-pitched sounds, bell for low (S3/S4). Bare skin, never through the gown. Watch with a second hand.
  • Collect accurate objective dataYour numbers should match the patient in front of you, not just the chart.

What this picture suggests

Focus: Cardiovascular. An older adult on a loop diuretic with fast breathing, borderline oxygen, and elevated BP fits fluid volume excess, such as heart failure. Your exam is looking for proof of fluid backing up: JVD, edema, crackles, an S3.

Chart traps to catch
  • The name is Patricia but the chart says Male. Verify identity with two identifiers and point out the mismatch.
  • Furosemide 40 mg every 4 hours PO is 240 mg/day. Oral furosemide is usually daily or BID. Clarify with the provider before giving.

Recognize cues: cardiovascular 5 pts

Do the core routine first (14 pts), then these.

  • Inspect for JVD and edema in BUE and BLEHead of bed 30–45°, head turned slightly left, look at the right neck. Distension above the clavicle is abnormal. Press 5 s over the dorsum of the foot, medial malleolus, and shin; grade the pitting 1+ to 4+. Check hands and sacrum too.
  • Capillary refill, and say the resultFingers and toes. "Cap refill under 2 seconds bilaterally."
  • Auscultate APETM; apical pulse for a full minuteAortic 2nd ICS right sternal border · Pulmonic 2nd ICS left SB · Erb's 3rd ICS left SB · Tricuspid 4th ICS lower left SB · Mitral 5th ICS midclavicular (apical). Diaphragm, then bell at the apex for an S3 ("Ken-tuck-y"), the classic heart-failure sound.
  • Say the rate, rhythm, and symmetry of pulsesRadials together: "Radial pulses 2+ and equal bilaterally, regular rhythm, apical 88."
  • Posterior tibial and dorsalis pedis pulsesPT sits behind the medial malleolus; DP on the top of the foot, lateral to the big-toe tendon. Compare both sides at once.
Bonus that supports your hypothesisListen to the posterior lung bases for crackles. RR 22 with SpO₂ 94% needs a lung explanation even on a cardiac scenario.

Analyze cues 20 pts

Normal (say these)
  • HR 88, temp 98.4 within range
  • A&O ×4, pain only 2/10
  • Whatever is normal on your exam, e.g. regular rhythm, 2+ pulses, cap refill <2 s
Abnormal (say these)
  • RR 22: tachypnea
  • SpO₂ 94%: below normal
  • BP 138/86: stage 1 hypertension
  • Any JVD, pitting edema, S3, crackles you find
Why it matters

When the heart can't pump forward, fluid backs up. Left-side failure pushes fluid into the lungs, which causes fast breathing, low SpO₂, and crackles. Right-side failure backs it into the body, which causes JVD and edema. High BP adds afterload and makes it worse.

What else you need
  • Daily weights and trend (gain >2–3 lb/day or 5 lb/week)
  • Intake & output
  • Potassium (normal 3.5–5.0 mEq/L), sodium (normal 135–145 mEq/L), BUN (normal 10–20 mg/dL) and creatinine (normal 0.6–1.2 mg/dL) before a loop diuretic
  • BNP (normal less than 100 pg/mL), chest X-ray, history of HF
  • Orthopnea: how many pillows? Dyspnea on exertion?

Prioritize hypotheses 20 pts

Priority problem: Fluid volume excess causing impaired gas exchange (RR 22, SpO₂ 94%). Breathing comes first (ABCs). Pain at 2/10 is not the priority.

Say it in SBAR

S
"I'm calling about Patricia Thompson, 72, room __. Respirations are 22 and SpO₂ is 94%."
B
"Admitted on oral furosemide 40 mg every 4 hours; no known allergies; full code."
A
"I found [JVD / 2+ pitting edema bilaterally / crackles at the bases]. BP 138/86. I'm concerned about fluid overload."
R
"Can you confirm the furosemide frequency? I'd also like orders for daily weights, strict I&O, a BMP for potassium, and O₂ to keep SpO₂ ≥ 94%."

Take action 10 pts

  • Raise the head of bed to semi- or high-Fowler's to ease breathing
  • Apply O₂ per protocol or order
  • Notify the provider (SBAR above) and clarify the furosemide order
  • Check potassium and BP before giving the diuretic
  • Start strict I&O, daily weight (same time, same scale), fluid and sodium limits if ordered
  • Elevate edematous legs; protect fragile skin

Evaluate outcomes 20 pts

Reassess

RR, SpO₂, lung sounds, BP, edema grade, urine output, weight, potassium.

Expected outcome

RR 12–20, SpO₂ ≥ 95%, clear lungs, less edema, good urine output within 1–2 h of the diuretic, weight trending down.

Escalate if

SpO₂ keeps dropping, RR climbs, pink frothy sputum, new confusion, SBP < 90, or K⁺ < 3.5. Call the provider or rapid response.

NURA 320 med pass: furosemide (Lasix)

Class & action

Loop diuretic. Blocks sodium and chloride reabsorption in the loop of Henle, so the kidneys excrete more water.

Check before giving
  • BP: hold and call if hypotensive (commonly SBP < 90)
  • Potassium (normal 3.5–5.0 mEq/L); furosemide wastes K⁺
  • Weight, I&O, lung sounds, edema
  • Rights of med admin; question the q4h frequency
Teach the patient
  • Expect to urinate more; give it in the morning so it doesn't disrupt sleep
  • Stand up slowly; you may feel dizzy
  • Eat potassium-rich foods (bananas, potatoes, oranges) unless told not to
  • Weigh yourself every morning and report a gain of 2–3 lb in a day or 5 lb in a week
  • Report muscle cramps, weakness, irregular heartbeat, or ringing in the ears

What this picture suggests

Focus: Respiratory. A young adult with tachypnea, tachycardia, and low SpO₂ on a rescue inhaler fits bronchospasm, such as an asthma exacerbation. He is the sickest of the four on paper.

Chart traps to catch
  • Height is listed as "178" with no unit. Say you would clarify it (178 cm fits the BMI).
  • Attending is "None". Know who you'd call: the covering provider or rapid response.
  • HR 112 before albuterol. Albuterol raises HR further, so recheck it afterwards. Hypoxia is driving the tachycardia, so don't withhold the inhaler for it.

Recognize cues: respiratory 5 pts

Do the core routine first (14 pts), then these.

  • Inspect respiratory rate and effort; note any difficultyCount a full 30–60 s without telling him. Look for accessory muscle use, nasal flaring, retractions, pursed lips, tripod position, and whether he can speak full sentences.
  • Inspect AP diameter, chest symmetry, positioningNormal AP:lateral ratio is about 1:2; 1:1 is barrel chest. Watch both sides rise equally. Note whether he is sitting up or leaning forward.
  • Auscultate both lung fields anteriorly, posteriorly, laterallyDiaphragm on bare skin, one full breath per spot, side to side in a ladder pattern. Start posterior. Name what you hear: clear, wheezes (expiratory whistling = narrowed airways), diminished, crackles.
  • Palpate trachea, chest expansion, tenderness, crepitus, wall abnormalitiesTrachea midline in the sternal notch. Thumbs at T9–T10 posteriorly; they should move apart about 5 cm equally. Crepitus feels like crackling under the skin (air leak).
Red flagA "quiet" chest in a struggling asthmatic means almost no air is moving. That is an emergency, not an improvement.

Analyze cues 20 pts

Normal (say these)
  • Temp 98.6: no sign of infection
  • BP essentially stable
  • A&O ×4, trachea midline, symmetric expansion (if found)
Abnormal (say these)
  • RR 24: tachypnea
  • SpO₂ 93%: hypoxemia
  • HR 112: tachycardia
  • Wheezes, accessory muscle use, short phrases (if found)
Why it matters

Bronchospasm narrows the airways, so less oxygen reaches the blood. The body compensates by breathing faster and raising the heart rate. If he tires out, RR can drop and CO₂ rises. That is respiratory failure.

What else you need
  • Asthma history, triggers, last rescue inhaler use, prior intubations
  • Peak flow compared with personal best
  • ABG if worsening: pH (normal 7.35–7.45), PaCO₂ (normal 35–45 mm Hg), PaO₂ (normal 75–100 mm Hg); chest X-ray
  • Sputum color, fever, recent illness
  • When his last albuterol dose was

Prioritize hypotheses 20 pts

Priority problem: Ineffective airway clearance and impaired gas exchange from bronchospasm (SpO₂ 93%, RR 24, HR 112). This is an Airway/Breathing problem, so it outranks everything.

Say it in SBAR

S
"I'm calling about Daniel Martinez, 21, room __. SpO₂ is 93% with respirations of 24 and heart rate 112."
B
"He has an order for albuterol 2 puffs every 4 hours; no known allergies."
A
"I hear [expiratory wheezes bilaterally] and he is [using accessory muscles / speaking in short phrases]. I think he is having bronchospasm."
R
"I'm giving his albuterol now and applying O₂. Please come evaluate him. Would you like a peak flow, ABG, or nebulizer order if he doesn't improve?"

Take action 10 pts

  • High-Fowler's or tripod position
  • Give scheduled albuterol (rescue bronchodilator) with a spacer if available
  • Apply O₂ per protocol to keep SpO₂ ≥ 92–94%
  • Stay calm and coach slow, pursed-lip breathing
  • Notify the provider; call rapid response if he worsens

Evaluate outcomes 20 pts

Reassess

SpO₂, RR, effort, lung sounds, HR, ability to speak. Albuterol acts in about 5–15 min, so recheck then.

Expected outcome

SpO₂ ≥ 95%, RR 12–20, fewer or no wheezes, speaks full sentences, HR trending down.

Escalate if

SpO₂ < 90%, no improvement after the dose, silent chest, confusion or drowsiness, cyanosis, can't speak. Call rapid response.

NURA 320 med pass: albuterol MDI

Class & action

Short-acting beta-2 agonist (SABA). Relaxes bronchial smooth muscle and opens the airways within minutes. It is the rescue inhaler.

Check before giving
  • HR, RR, SpO₂, lung sounds (your baseline for evaluating it)
  • Last dose time; q4h means not before 1200 if it was given at 0800
  • Rights of med admin; route is inhaled
Teach the patient (MDI technique)
  • Shake the inhaler; attach the spacer
  • Breathe all the way out, seal lips around the mouthpiece
  • Press once as you start a slow, deep breath in
  • Hold for 10 seconds, then breathe out slowly
  • Wait 1 minute before the second puff
  • Side effects: shakiness, racing heart, nervousness
  • If you need it more often than prescribed, tell your provider; asthma may be getting worse

What this picture suggests

Focus: Neurological (by elimination: the other three cover CV, respiratory, and MSK). Stable vitals, moderate pain, and acetaminophen point to a headache work-up. Your exam has to rule out anything dangerous, like a stroke or rising intracranial pressure, before calling it simple pain.

Chart traps to catch
  • Weight "152" has no unit. Say you would clarify lb vs kg; it changes dosing math.
  • Acetaminophen 650 mg q4h is up to 3.9 g/day, right at the 4 g ceiling. Ask about other acetaminophen products (cold medicine, Norco/Percocet).
  • If it turns out to be MSK pain, use the Bennett tab's MSK checklist.

Recognize cues: neurological 5 pts

Do the core routine first (14 pts), then these.

  • LOC, orientation, and speechPerson, place, time, situation. Listen for slurring, word-finding trouble, or confusion. "Alert, oriented ×4, speech clear and appropriate."
  • Pupils: PERRLADim light; note size (2–6 mm) and shape. Swing a penlight in from the side; check direct and consensual response. Accommodation: pupils constrict and converge as a finger moves toward the nose.
  • Hand grips and foot pushes; upper and lower strengthBoth sides at once, compare. Grade 0–5. "Hand grips and pedal pushes strong and equal, 5/5 bilaterally." Asymmetry is the key finding.
  • Cranial nerves III, IV, VI: EOMsHold her chin still, trace an "H" (six cardinal gazes) about 12 in. from her face. Watch for smooth tracking, nystagmus, and lid lag. III oculomotor, IV trochlear, VI abducens.
  • Light-touch sensation bilaterallyEyes closed. Cotton ball or fingertip on arms, hands, legs, feet, same spots both sides. "Do you feel this? Is it the same on both sides?"
Stroke red flags: BE FASTBalance loss, Eyes (vision change), Face droop, Arm drift or weakness, Speech change, Time: note last known well. Any of these means call a stroke alert and keep her NPO (nothing by mouth, including the acetaminophen) until a swallow screen.

Analyze cues 20 pts

Normal (say these)
  • HR, RR, temp, SpO₂ all within range
  • A&O ×4, PERRLA, EOMs intact, equal strength and sensation (if found)
Abnormal (say these)
  • Pain 5/10: moderate
  • BP 132/82: stage 1 hypertension
  • Any unequal pupils, weakness, numbness, slurred speech
Why it matters

With a normal neuro exam, this is acute pain that affects comfort and rest. Raised BP can come from pain or contribute to headache. Any one-sided deficit or pupil change suggests stroke or rising intracranial pressure, which is time-critical.

What else you need
  • Headache history: worst ever? sudden "thunderclap"?
  • Vision changes, nausea or vomiting, neck stiffness, fever
  • Recent fall or head injury; anticoagulant use
  • Last acetaminophen dose and total in 24 h; liver disease, alcohol use
  • Trend BP; blood glucose (normal 70–99 mg/dL; low sugar can mimic stroke)

Prioritize hypotheses 20 pts

Priority problem: Acute pain (headache 5/10). Escalate to risk for ineffective cerebral perfusion if you find any focal deficit. Say both: "If my neuro exam is normal, my priority is pain. If I found a deficit, it becomes possible stroke and I'd call immediately."

Take action 10 pts

  • Give acetaminophen as ordered after confirming the 24-h total
  • Non-drug comfort: dim lights, quiet room, cool cloth, repositioning
  • Raise head of bed slightly; keep the call light in reach
  • If any deficit: stroke alert or rapid response, NPO, note time last known well, notify provider

Evaluate outcomes 20 pts

Reassess

Pain score 30–60 min after the oral dose; repeat neuro checks; BP.

Expected outcome

Pain at or below her acceptable goal (e.g. ≤ 3/10), neuro exam unchanged and intact, BP trending down as pain eases.

Escalate if

Pain unrelieved or worsening, new neuro deficit, confusion, vomiting, stiff neck, or sudden severe headache. Notify the provider right away.

NURA 320 med pass: acetaminophen (Tylenol)

Class & action

Non-opioid analgesic and antipyretic. Reduces pain and fever; no anti-inflammatory effect and no bleeding risk.

Check before giving
  • Pain score and when the last dose was given
  • Total acetaminophen in 24 h from all sources: max 4 g (many facilities use 3 g in older adults or liver risk)
  • Liver disease, heavy alcohol use
  • Can she swallow safely? Neuro intact?
Teach the patient
  • Many cold and pain products also contain acetaminophen; read labels
  • Avoid alcohol while taking it
  • Report yellow skin or eyes, dark urine, or right upper belly pain
  • Tell the nurse if pain isn't better in about an hour

What this picture suggests

Focus: Musculoskeletal. An older adult with the highest pain score of the four, stable vitals, and an NSAID ordered fits joint pain, such as osteoarthritis or a joint injury. Pain limits mobility, and limited mobility in a 71-year-old means fall risk.

Chart traps to catch
  • Attending is "None". Know who you'd notify.
  • Ibuprofen in an adult over 65 is on the Beers list for GI bleeding and kidney injury, and NSAIDs can raise BP (his is 132/84). Ask about stomach ulcers, kidney disease, and blood thinners.
  • 400 mg q4h = 2,400 mg/day: within the prescription maximum (3,200 mg) but above the OTC limit.

Recognize cues: musculoskeletal 5 pts

Do the core routine first (14 pts), then these.

  • Inspect the affected joint for swelling or rednessExpose it and compare to the other side. Look for swelling, redness, deformity, bruising. Feel for warmth with the back of your hand. A hot, red, swollen joint suggests infection or gout.
  • Assess range of motionActive first ("Can you bend your knee for me?"), then gentle passive. Stop at pain and never force. Note crepitus (grating) and how far he gets.
  • Pain assessment: location, quality, intensity, timingOPQRST specific to the joint: worse with movement or at rest? Morning stiffness lasting <30 min suggests osteoarthritis.
  • Functional mobility: gait or transfer ability, if appropriateGait belt on, bed low, non-skid socks. Dangle first. Watch balance, stride, and whether he needs a walker or cane. If he's unsteady, say you would not ambulate him alone.

Red flag: the 6 P'sPain out of proportion, pallor, pulselessness, paresthesia, paralysis, poikilothermia (cold). These mean neurovascular compromise or compartment syndrome. Notify the provider immediately.

Analyze cues 20 pts

Normal (say these)
  • HR, RR, temp, SpO₂ within range: no sign of infection or distress
  • CMS intact (warm, pink, cap refill <2 s, moves and feels all extremities), if found
Abnormal (say these)
  • Pain 6/10: moderate, highest of the four patients
  • BP 132/84: stage 1 hypertension (pain can raise it)
  • Any swelling, redness, warmth, limited ROM, crepitus, unsteady gait
Why it matters

Pain limits movement. Limited movement plus age 71 raises the risk of falls, stiffness, pressure injury, and clots. A warm, red joint could mean infection. Any CMS change means blood flow or nerves are compromised.

What else you need
  • Cause: injury, arthritis, recent surgery, gout history
  • X-ray results; CBC with WBC (normal 5,000–10,000/mm³), ESR (normal female 0–20, male 0–15 mm/hr), and CRP (normal less than 1.0 mg/dL) if the joint is hot
  • Kidney function before an NSAID: BUN (normal 10–20 mg/dL), creatinine (normal 0.6–1.2 mg/dL)
  • History of GI bleeding or ulcers; anticoagulants or aspirin
  • Fall history; home mobility aids

Priority problem: Acute pain (6/10) impairing physical mobility, with high fall risk. If CMS is abnormal, the priority becomes neurovascular compromise.

Evaluate outcomes 20 pts

Reassess

Pain 30–60 min after the dose, ROM, gait and transfers, CMS, BP, GI symptoms.

Expected outcome

Pain at or below his goal (e.g. ≤ 3/10), moves the joint and transfers safely, CMS stays intact, no falls.

Escalate if

Pain unrelieved, new numbness or coolness, increasing swelling, fever, black or tarry stools, or reduced urine output.

NURA 320 med pass: ibuprofen (Motrin, Advil)

Class & action

NSAID. Blocks COX enzymes, so it lowers prostaglandins: less pain, inflammation, and fever.

Check before giving
  • Pain score and last dose time
  • History of GI bleeding or ulcers, kidney disease, heart failure
  • Anticoagulants, aspirin, steroids, other NSAIDs
  • BP; kidney labs if available
Teach the patient
  • Take with food or milk and a full glass of water
  • Don't combine with other NSAIDs (naproxen, aspirin) or alcohol
  • Report black or tarry stools, vomiting that looks like coffee grounds, stomach pain
  • Report swelling, less urine, or sudden weight gain
  • Get up slowly and call for help before walking
